I want to study for a Master of Science in Computer Science at AUC because the systems I build keep raising research questions I cannot yet answer properly. How do you know a language model system is getting better, and not just different? Why do tools built for English work worse in Arabic, and what would it take to measure and fix that? A thesis-based master's is the right place to answer those questions with the rigour they need.

My path into research started during my BSc in Computer Science at Culture and Science City, which I completed in 2024. As an undergraduate I published two papers. With a colleague, I fine-tuned Stable Diffusion with LoRA on about 12,000 captioned floor plans to generate furnished layouts from text, work published in *Machine Graphics & Vision* (2025). As a remote research intern with QWorld's UAE chapter, I built a quantum machine-learning pipeline that used a variational autoencoder to denoise EEG signals for brain-computer interfaces, presented at the Neurogenomics 2024 Conference. Both projects taught me the same lesson: an idea is only as good as the evidence behind it.

Since graduating, I have applied that lesson to large language model systems. My main project is a conversational shopping assistant over a catalogue of 12,730 products. I treated it as an experiment rather than a demo. I labelled 127 queries and measured retrieval with 95% confidence intervals: hybrid search found the right product in the top ten 72% of the time, against 56% for keyword search and 41% for vector search alone. Reranking raised the share of queries with the right product in first place from 23% to 43%. I designed a code-graded evaluation of the agent, with 12 tasks each run three times. It showed that a revised prompt solved all 12 tasks instead of 7, and that moving price and rating limits inside the search step raised the share of matching results from 70% to 100%. I kept a log of every change and its measured effect, which is the habit I want to carry into a thesis.
